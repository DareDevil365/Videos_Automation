# Finding formats in other markets and adapting them for a US-first audience

Checked: **2026-11-01**. Research method and candidate profiles only. No accounts, Trends API access, regional dataset, media downloads, production tests, uploads or cross-market audience results were created. The user's current preference is **US-first** for both existing channels. UK, Canadian and Australian results can be optional benchmarks; they are not substitutes for demonstrating US reach.

## Strategy and present evidence

The proposed strategy is to discover an effective storytelling or teaching mechanism in one language/cultural context, look for a specific unmet need among US viewers, and create an original adaptation. It is a useful discovery hypothesis. It does not establish that formats reliably spread country by country, that the US is always later, or that importing a format gives first-mover advantage.

The existing project evidence includes an Indian-film sigma example with substantial reach, an English AI-tool cost-saving Short with substantial reach, English chat/comeback examples, and several much smaller examples. None supplies the competitor's actual viewer-country breakdown. **No origin-market → US diffusion sequence or untapped US market has yet been demonstrated.** The six profiles below deliberately distinguish observations from opportunity hypotheses, including cases where English packaging is already well established.

Use this method across the existing catalogs; it does not require inventing another set of format IDs or launching more channels. Entertainment stays separate from educational/productivity content. Reference records: [entertainment evidence](../formats/entertainment/ENTERTAINMENT_PERFORMANCE_EVIDENCE.md), [education evidence](../formats/education-and-movies/EDUCATION_PERFORMANCE_EVIDENCE.md), [direct reference checks](../references/DIRECT_REFERENCE_CHECKS.md), [US-first plan](US_FIRST_REVENUE_PLAN.md).

## What “market” means in a research record

| Field | Evidence that can support it | What must not be inferred |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spoken/written language | Directly inspected audio/captions/text, with inspection limits | English title alone does not establish spoken English. |
| Cultural/source context | Observable setting, references, topic, currency, school system, creator's explicit positioning | An Indian actor or Hindi caption does not establish that viewers are all in India. |
| Intended audience | Explicit creator statement or a narrow source/brief targeting a group | Stated intent is not proof of delivered reach. |
| Creator operation/residence | Reliable owner-supplied information where relevant | Account/channel country and physical residence are not audience geography. |
| Actual viewer geography | Authorized owned-channel reports, or sufficiently scoped owner-published analytics | Public view counts, language, comments and search filters do not reveal country shares. |
| Market gap | A dated, disclosed competition search plus an identifiable unsatisfied need, followed by a test | Few search results do not prove no competition, no demand, or first arrival. |

Keep unknown fields unknown. A video can be strongly associated with a cultural context and simultaneously reach a global audience.

## An actionable discovery-to-test workflow

### 1. Define the mechanism and destination need

Describe the format without its borrowed assets: “a boast, a brief pause, a clever reply and an explanatory payoff” or “a visible software task, one change and a checked output.” Identify the US viewer's concrete problem or entertainment expectation. Separate the topic, hook, layout, narrative mechanism, cultural reference and revenue route.

Example research question: “Would a clearly fictional English group-chat misunderstanding work for US college-age viewers?” This is testable. “Indian sigma videos are new in America” is not supported by the current record.

### 2. Search source-language contexts without declaring their audiences

Choose a small rotating set of language/context queries: Hindi chat/reply edits, Spanish study demonstrations, Portuguese story jokes, or English job/AI demonstrations. These are **discovery seeds**, not findings that one country invented those formats. Use terms a fluent speaker would actually use; machine translation should be treated as a draft and checked for idiom. Do not stereotype a whole country from a few channels.

For each promising family, find multiple distinct episodes from at least two channels where possible. Include ordinary/low-count examples, recent and older examples, and content that contradicts the first impression. Save exact URLs, published dates, observed raw counters, collection time, counter source, language/format inspection, and missing information. If a source is uninspectable, record title-level evidence only.

### 3. Test whether the source signal is actually current

An old hit proves accumulated reach, not recent growth. A single recent hit can be an exception. Check whether the same mechanism appears in several recent distinct episodes, whether it relies on an established personality, and whether the supposed new form already has older versions. Distinguish the creator's own audience, an authorized clipping campaign and third-party edits.

Do not calculate custom API-based growth/virality scores or invent audience composition. Use allowed raw official fields and editorial observations under the source policy. A campaign listing, rate or budget is evidence of a commercial program, not proof of a format's growing popularity.

### 4. Search the destination before calling it an opportunity

Use US-relevant English synonyms, descriptions of the viewer's need, and descriptions of the mechanism—not only a literal translation of the foreign name. Search recent/date-ordered references as well as relevance-ranked results and directly inspect selected Shorts where accessible. Add native English brands/creators already covering the subject. Optional UK/CA/AU searches can reveal English-language competition that also reaches US viewers.

For each candidate, record: exact query, search surface/filter, time window, date, accessible results, inspected matches, counterexamples and limitations. Classify the result as **established competition**, **possible narrow gap**, **insufficient evidence**, or **no useful source signal yet**. These are transparent editorial judgments, not statistical market scores.

A narrow gap might be “existing examples do not show this beginner task on a genuinely free plan,” “the explanation assumes local exam knowledge,” or “the story has no complete payoff.” Those gaps need inspection, not just title matching. Search personalization, spelling, language and ranking can hide competitors; absence in our results is not absence on YouTube.

### 5. Check search interest as supplementary evidence

Use the Google Trends website where accessible, preserving the actual query/topic, geography, date range, category and search property. Compare a candidate against relevant reference queries in the **same comparison setup**, rather than independently normalizing every chart and comparing their peaks. Run US checks first and use source-context/UK/CA/AU checks as supporting comparisons.

Where available, use the YouTube Search property for a YouTube-search question and label it separately from Web Search. Search interest can help with an education topic, but feed-driven chat comedy or visual humor may have little named search demand. A searchable topic is not the same thing as a Shorts layout. No Trends chart or regional numeric result was collected in this pass.

### 6. Design a genuine US adaptation

Rewrite the premise, dialogue, examples, references, pacing and explanation for the chosen audience. Adapt résumé/job conventions, tool availability, units/currency, exam names, holidays and humor only when they are relevant and verified. A natural English line is more useful than a mechanically translated joke. Do not invent a US identity, accent credentials, employment experience, earnings or geography claims.

Use an originality brief stating what we learned from the reference and what our episode newly supplies. Acquire or generate our own assets unless an exact permitted source is verified. A translated script, dubbed clip, redrawn copyrighted character or recreated private conversation is not automatically cleared merely because the audience changes.

### 7. Build a cheap, bounded experiment

First make a private feasibility sample, using the existing local/free-only plan. Then, if publication is separately authorized, test a small set of materially different episodes from the same narrow series. A proposed starting batch is three original episodes per selected series, with a second batch only if results justify continuing. This is a budget/learning choice, not a statistical sample-size guarantee.

Hold the core teaching/story mechanism reasonably stable and document intentional variations. Do not repost the same episode with swapped language or names as an uncontrolled mass-upload test. Save the hook, intended viewer, adaptation choices, assets, claim proofs, render effort and review result. A single success or failure should prompt another hypothesis, not a declaration that a country loves or rejects the format.

### 8. Measure actual destination delivery

Use our authorized official country breakdowns and available metrics in Studio/Analytics. Check US reach together with engaged viewing, retention or viewing duration where supported, subscriber response and the relevant revenue action. Do not create a custom country-share metric from API data unless the applicable use is permitted; prefer official reported geography/breakdowns. Mark delayed, low-volume or unavailable country data as unknown, not zero. [Official channel reports](https://developers.google.com/youtube/analytics/channel_reports).

Review comparable observation windows, such as 24 hours, 72 hours and seven days, consistent with the existing pilot plan. Viewers outside the US are not automatically a failure; distinguish audience fit from a specific campaign's geography requirement. Do not extrapolate RPM, sponsor demand or conversions from a US-looking title. Monetization and costs require their own actual records.

## How to interpret Google Trends without inventing a market gap

- UI values are relative, normalized **0–100**, not search counts. Equal regional values do not imply equal absolute audiences. A low-volume zero is not proof that nobody searches or that a market is empty. Sampling and filters matter. [Official data FAQ](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533?hl=en).
- Terms use the selected wording/language; spelling variants and synonyms require care. Topics group a concept across languages where an appropriate topic exists. Do not compare a narrowly spelled local term with a broad English topic as if the definitions match. [Term/topic comparison guidance](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4359550?hl=en).
- Keep time window, property, category and geography visible. An independently rescaled 100 in one chart is not evidence of the same volume as 100 in another. A short spike can be news-driven; sustained interest and destination competition still need investigation.
- A query's interest is evidence about that query/concept, not a direct measurement of format supply, feed engagement, creator earnings or advertiser value. Trends is not a poll of all YouTube viewers.
- The public site supports CSV export and citation, subject to its terms. The agent can perform permitted accessible research or record an unavailable step; the user should not be tasked with collecting every chart. [Official export instructions](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365538?hl=en).
- The official Trends API remains an **application-gated alpha** at this check. Its consistently scaled API data has different scaling from the UI. No access, unrestricted free quota or automation capability is assumed. Do not scrape restricted endpoints or bypass controls. [Official API access page](https://developers.google.com/search/apis/trends).

## YouTube search, location and global-audience constraints

The official `search.list` documentation says `regionCode=US` returns videos **viewable** in the US; it does not reveal videos whose viewers are primarily American. `relevanceLanguage=en` favors relevant language results but can include other languages. `videoDuration=short` means less than four minutes, not verified Shorts. Confirm the actual Shorts interface/runtime when necessary. [Search parameter definitions](https://developers.google.com/youtube/data/reference/rest/v3/search/list).

The current quota page lists a default 100 search calls/day in a separate search bucket, plus separate upload/other-endpoint allowances. A first discovery run should use a bounded queue and deduplicate references; for example, cap research at 24 search requests before deciding whether another pass is useful. This is a proposed internal budget, not a service promise or a request for more quota. No keys/projects are rotated to bypass limits. [Current quota reference](https://developers.google.com/youtube/data/determine_quota_cost).

YouTube states channel-location settings are not used to determine recommendations; its discovery guidance emphasizes viewer response and relevance. No VPN, fake location, upload time or language tag guarantees US distribution. [Official recommendation FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/141805?hl=en).

YouTube describes one multilingual channel, separate language channels and global/local combinations as options, each with management tradeoffs. Keep the existing two-channel plan initially; do not launch a channel for every candidate market before proving production and demand. Later localization can use available supported features or a separate channel when audience/maintenance evidence warrants it. [Global-audience guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6070467?hl=en).

## Six transfer candidate profiles

The observations below come from the project's dated research and direct-check records. No profile presently proves source-country view share, US scarcity or a chronological migration. They are prioritized research/adaptation questions, not newly validated formats.

### 1. Contextual comeback / sigma mechanism → understandable US entertainment

**Source observation:** the coordinator directly sampled the [Shahrukh Khan reply Short](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/5FzNT-oyDEk) and read 66,627,969 views on 2026-11-01. It uses Indian-film celebrity event footage and prominent captions. That establishes cultural context and one outcome, not an India-only audience or an untouched US opportunity. Another similarly titled example had far fewer indexed views.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** a setup, controlled pause and sharp reply could work with a US-comprehensible situation. Start with an original fictional character/chat, or a genuinely eligible US-relevant creator moment with original explanation. Translate the mechanism rather than transplant an unfamiliar celebrity's apparent status. Do not fabricate a real creator's reply or add a famous “sigma” track without permission.

**Gap check:** search English `comeback`, `witty reply`, `sigma reply`, `aura` and context-specific synonyms across recent Shorts; inspect actual setups/payoffs and low-result examples. Look for a narrow storytelling difference, not an alleged absence of sigma content. Source/media/music rights and the user's higher-risk edit choices remain pending.

### 2. Fictional chat/storytime → US-specific original mini-story

**Source observation:** [Beluga's comeback reference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QPwTKSMcAL8) had 10,012,982 indexed views in the earlier report; its exact structure/runtime was not inspected. [Subway Surfers storytime](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DSAo9FmfiD8) is an older, modest-count reference from 2020. English comeback/storytime packaging already exists; the format is not established as untapped English content.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** original fictional group-chat or workplace/college misunderstandings could fit a narrow US-viewer situation. Write new dialogue and a complete ending. Use a generated generic chat interface and original motion background; if gameplay is used, verify the game and recording rights separately.

**Gap check:** search the actual scenario and narrative mechanism alongside broad `texting story`, `chat comedy` and `storytime` terms. Inspect whether comparable recent stories already deliver the same joke. Borrowing Reddit text, another writer's conversation or another recorder's gameplay is not cleared by localization. The experiment is distinct writing/audience fit, not importing an allegedly missing category.

### 3. Hindi film recommendation presentation → US viewing-decision help

**Source observation:** the coordinator sampled [Movie Recommendation](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/LHayWSJTdOA): moving film footage, Hindi captions/narration context, and 62 directly observed views. It does not demonstrate a winning source-market recipe. The exact nine-film/poster-GIF/viewer-tenth variant remains unverified in the catalog.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** test a narrowly useful recommendation such as an original spoiler-free explanation of who a film suits and why. US streaming availability must be checked on the actual service at publication; a title available in one country may not be available in another. An imported obscure title can be an editorial choice, not a proven market gap.

**Gap check:** search the film/genre plus `worth watching`, `movies like`, `underrated` and relevant viewing situations. Inspect English recommendation supply and whether it already answers the need. Posters, GIFs, film scenes, music, metadata commercial terms and recommendation substantiation are separate unresolved rights/data questions. Keep the minimal verdict/list versions available for the user's risk decision, but do not call the sampled low-count video a source-market success.

### 4. Free-AI task demonstration → a narrow US beginner workflow

**Source observation:** the coordinator directly checked Kevin Stratvert's [cost-saving AI Short](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/1fA5j5_5TRY) at 4,700,231 views. The sample had a paid-promotion label and a related tutorial. English competition with substantial reach already exists. The record also includes AI/earning-promise examples at 27 and 252 indexed views.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** show one real free-plan task with a tangible output and an honest limitation, using a US-relevant fictional scenario such as organizing job-search notes. If a different-language tutorial suggests a better explanation, rebuild the demonstration and script independently. Verify current access, commercial-use rights and data handling.

**Gap check:** search the exact task, current tool/version, `free`, and beginner constraints; compare existing free-vs-paid explanations. The plausible opening is a specific unanswered task or restriction, not “AI tools have not reached America.” Paid feature promotion must not quietly become a paid production dependency. Affiliate/sponsor access and disclosures remain separate.

### 5. Local study-explanation mechanism → a US learning task

**Source observation:** [Let'stute's study-tip reference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gZm2-vae5DY) had 2,184,681 indexed views and a `#shorts` title. Language, runtime and viewer geography were uninspected. English study advice is already established; the title's “90%” assertion was not independently supported.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** use an original question, retrieval pause and corrective feedback for a clearly stated subject. Where local exam terminology motivated a source example, substitute an accurately researched US learner task rather than promise equivalent grades across systems. Technique evidence should come from the study research, not a source video's counter.

**Gap check:** search the exact subject/learning difficulty and `practice question`, `active recall` or `study example`, with US-relevant exam terms only when appropriate. Inspect whether the gap is a missing worked example rather than a missing technique. Do not translate copyrighted test items or assert a universal timetable. This is an original teaching experiment with uncertain audience response.

### 6. Interview/communication lesson → US job-seeker application

**Source observation:** two English-packaged `tell me about yourself` references had 158,638 and 10,597 indexed views in the education report: [The Urban Fight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BFWUOZX5kI8) and [Enterprise Mobility Careers Europe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IqrVumjorps). Neither supplies actual viewer geography. This is not a missing English subject.

**US hypothesis/adaptation:** a clearly fictional before/after answer could help one specific audience, such as a first-job applicant with an honest practice project. Adapt résumé wording and job requirements using employer/official guidance; avoid replacing real experience with impressive invented results. Confidence content should rehearse a specific respectful sentence, not sell a universal psychological transformation.

**Gap check:** search the role, experience level and question, not just broad job advice. Inspect whether existing answers assume senior experience or omit evidence viewers can honestly supply. Verify geographic hiring requirements and source dates before mentioning an opening. No job, salary or income guarantee is justified. Services, paid guides or template sales require their own support/operator plan.

## Research record and decision gates

For each candidate save: existing `format_id`, exact source URLs, observed language/context, actual audience evidence or `unknown`, publication/collection dates, counter provenance, inspected mechanism, proposed US viewer, destination search log, English competitors, evidence for/against a narrow gap, Trends query/filter record or `not_collected`, adaptation brief, exact asset rights status, free dependencies, claim proof, pilot plan, and user risk decision.

Use plain decisions:

- **Research further:** mechanism is interesting but source performance or US competition is unclear.
- **Pilot candidate:** an original adaptation is feasible and has a concrete US need to test; this is not publication approval or proof of a gap.
- **Established English competition:** pursue only with a useful distinct angle, not a first-arrival claim.
- **Hold:** missing media rights, factual evidence, supported free access or appropriate review prevents production/publication as applicable.
