# Comedy: mechanism, escalation and timing

Find a recognizable want under pressure: avoid embarrassment, sound competent, save effort, win an argument. Give the other person a different priority. Their sensible responses should make the first person's solution worse. Useful mechanisms include literal interpretation, status reversal, overconfident commitment, mutually incompatible goals, and a callback whose meaning changes. Random objects and surreal claims are options only when the premise makes them pay off.

Plant enough ordinary information to make the ending retrospectively fair. Withhold the interpretation, not all the necessary facts. Escalate consequences rather than volume: the same bad decision reaches a new person, costs something new, or removes an escape. Repeated "what?", "bro" and disbelief are not three separate developments.

Place the decisive comic word or thought near the end of its message. Do not append the speaker's explanation. Let the audience connect setup and payoff. A short reply after a long justification can create a useful contrast; a callback works when the old phrase now means something different. A rule of three is optional, and the third beat must change the pattern rather than merely repeat it.

For timing, avoid a uniform long typing delay before all lines. Keep connective replies quick. Reserve one modest anticipation beat before a major reversal; then allow enough time to hear and read the result. Silence can let an implication land, while an immediate panicked correction can be funnier than a delay. Choose based on the character's intention. Our initial editor bounds are practical defaults, not scientifically optimal comedy timing. Never promise that a pause or a popular music cue makes the joke funny.

Failure check: without the title and music, can a reader name the expectation, the surprising turn and why that turn follows from these characters' choices? If not, rebuild before rendering.
